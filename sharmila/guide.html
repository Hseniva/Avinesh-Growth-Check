Day 1 · Module 1: Strategic Marketing & Sales Excellence

Marketing and sales work best as one connected engine aimed at shared business outcomes, not as two teams passing leads over a wall.

What you will learn

  • How marketing and sales together drive growth and commercial performance

  • How to align both functions with company goals and customer expectations

  • How the customer journey works and where decisions are won or lost

  • How to spot market opportunities and build a real competitive advantage

  • What drives sales performance, engagement and conversion

Key ideas

  • The landscape has changed. Customers arrive informed and expect credibility, relevance and value. Digital channels and data change how you reach them, and the shift is from one-off transactions to long-term relationships.

  • Shared objectives. Marketing builds awareness, demand and qualified leads. Sales converts them by showing value and building relationships. Both own revenue, acquisition, conversion and retention. Misalignment shows up as weak leads, mixed messages and lost deals.

  • The customer journey. Awareness, consideration, evaluation, purchase, relationship. Each stage has different needs, and strong awareness with weak conversion points you to the consideration and decision stages.

  • Market opportunities come from unmet needs, trends, technology, regulation, customer feedback and competitor gaps. Judge them on demand, potential, competition and your capability.

  • Competitive advantage is customer value that competitors cannot easily copy, based on what customers value rather than what you believe internally. Competing on price alone hurts profit and loyalty.

  • Four growth routes: market penetration (more share with current customers), market development (new segments or regions), offering development (new or improved solutions) and account development (retention, cross-selling, upselling).

  • Diagnose before you push. Poor results usually come from targeting, engagement, capability or process. Fix the root cause rather than adding more leads or pressure.

Tool: five strategic questions

AreaQuestion to ask
CustomerWho are our most valuable customers, and what drives their decisions?
ValueWhat problems do we solve, and how much do they matter to the customer?
OpportunityWhere are the strongest growth opportunities, and what evidence supports them?
DifferentiationWhy should customers choose us over competitors or alternatives?
ImprovementWhat should marketing and sales change to improve engagement, conversion and retention?

Practice

Think of your own organisation. Map its customer journey in five stages and write one customer need at each stage. Then name two actions that would bring your marketing and sales teams closer, such as a joint monthly review of lead quality or a shared definition of a qualified lead.

Self-check

  1. Your campaigns create strong awareness but few purchases. Which part of the journey do you examine first? Consideration and decision-making.

  2. What makes a competitive advantage sustainable? Value customers find meaningful that competitors find hard to copy.

Day 1 · Module 2: Market Intelligence & Customer Insights

Market intelligence turns information about customers, competitors and trends into decisions about who to target, what to say and where to focus.

What you will learn

  • How market intelligence supports targeting and strategic decisions

  • How to segment customers by needs, behaviour, priorities and value

  • How to build evidence-based customer personas

  • How to identify needs, expectations, pain points and buying motives

  • How to prioritise the opportunities with the strongest business value

Key ideas

  • Sources of intelligence: customer feedback, sales conversations, market research, competitor activity and industry developments. Information becomes intelligence only when it changes a decision.

  • Market analysis and customer analysis work together. Market analysis covers size, demand, competitors, trends, opportunities and risks. Customer analysis covers needs, priorities, buying behaviour, motivations and decision factors. Numbers show what is happening; conversations explain why.

  • Segmentation groups customers who share characteristics: demographic (size, industry, role), behavioural (purchasing patterns, loyalty), needs-based (problems, desired outcomes) and value-based (revenue potential, lifetime value).

  • Personas are evidence-based profiles of a target customer: context, goals, challenges, buying triggers, objections and decision factors. Build them from research, not assumptions, and review them as markets change.

  • Stated needs versus underlying needs. Customers often state a requirement without the reason behind it. Good questions and active listening uncover the real need.

  • Pain points can be operational (delays, cost), strategic (growth, competition), financial (wasted spend, unclear value) or emotional (fear of a wrong decision). Prioritise them by urgency and business impact.

  • Buying decisions mix rational factors (price, quality, results), emotional factors (trust, reputation, perceived risk) and organisational factors (several stakeholders with different priorities).

Tool: opportunity scoring

Score each prospect from 1 to 5 on the four criteria below and add the scores. Look at them together: a large customer with a weak need is not a high-value opportunity.

CriterionWhat to look for
Commercial valueRevenue, profitability, lifetime value
Customer fitCan we genuinely meet their needs?
Growth potentialRepeat or expanded business over time
Strategic importanceCredibility, market access, referrals

Practice

Pick one product or service. Define two meaningful customer segments, then write a short persona for one of them: role, goals, challenges, buying motives and decision factors. List three pain points and how each could influence the decision.

Self-check

  1. A customer keeps delaying because implementation looks complicated. Is that a buying motive or a pain point? A pain point.

  2. Why validate personas regularly? Customers in the same segment still differ, and markets change.

Day 1 · Module 3: Value Proposition & Market Positioning

A value proposition explains the specific value you create for a target customer and why your offer deserves their attention. Positioning makes that value clear and distinctive compared with the alternatives.

What you will learn

  • How to build a value proposition that links needs, benefits and outcomes

  • The difference between features, advantages, benefits and outcomes

  • How to differentiate products and services in ways customers value

  • How to position against competitors without criticising them

  • How to move beyond price-based selling

  • How to support value with credible proof

Key ideas

  • Features, advantages, benefits, outcomes. Features describe what the offer has or does. Advantages explain the improvement a feature brings. Benefits are what the customer gains. Outcomes are the measurable results. Good communication moves from what it does to why it matters.

  • A compelling proposition starts with the customer's needs and challenges, names the problem you solve best, shows how the solution improves their situation, uses the customer's language, and is tested and refined with feedback.

  • Differentiation counts only when customers value it and competitors cannot easily match it. It can come from expertise, quality, innovation, service, speed, reliability, customisation or results.

  • Positioning answers three questions quickly: who you serve, what problem you solve, and why it matters. Keep it consistent across marketing, sales, proposals and customer experience.

  • Beyond price. Price matters more when value is unclear. Help customers weigh cost against quality, reliability, risk and expected return.

  • Value differs by stakeholder. Senior leaders look for strategic and financial value, operations for usability and efficiency, finance for cost and return, technical teams for capability and security. Adapt the message, keep the proposition.

  • Proof builds trust: customer results, case studies, data, demonstrations and certifications, matched to the customer's situation. Specific results beat broad claims.

Tool: value proposition builder

StepYour answer
Target customerWho exactly is this for?
ProblemWhat challenge do they face, in their words?
SolutionHow does your offer address it?
OutcomeWhat measurable result do they get?
DifferenceWhy you rather than the alternatives?
ProofWhat evidence makes it believable?

Practice

Take three features of your product or service and turn each into a customer benefit and an outcome. Then write a two-sentence value proposition for one target customer and name one point of difference that matters to them.

Self-check

  1. When does a feature become a differentiator? When customers consider it important and competitors cannot easily match it.

  2. A customer focuses only on price. What might that signal? Uncertainty about value, budget limits, competitor comparisons or perceived risk.

Day 2 · Module 4: Strategic Prospecting & Opportunity Development

Strategic prospecting means finding the right customers, not simply more leads, and turning them into real opportunities through disciplined qualification and follow-up.

What you will learn

  • How strategic prospecting builds a healthy, consistent pipeline

  • How to identify ideal prospects by fit, need, value and likelihood to convert

  • Targeted prospecting techniques across channels and touchpoints

  • How to qualify leads and separate real opportunities from low-potential contacts

  • How to map decision-makers, influencers and users

  • How to prioritise opportunities and follow up in a structured way

Key ideas

  • Quality over volume. Align prospecting with your ideal customer profile and look for prospects with a meaningful problem you can solve.

  • The ideal prospect fits on industry, size, location, growth stage and budget, and also has a real business need, growth potential and strategic alignment. Review your criteria as markets change.

  • Prospecting techniques: account research (challenges, priorities, buying triggers), professional networks and referrals, digital signals of changing needs, and personalised outreach with a clear reason to engage.

  • Qualify early and keep qualifying on need, fit, timing, budget, authority, urgency and value, because buying conditions change.

  • Map the decision structure. Decision-makers hold authority; influencers shape opinion; users, technical, finance and procurement teams judge on different criteria.

  • Pipeline discipline. Stage opportunities on evidence, not optimism. Review quality, timing, risks and next steps regularly, and remove stale deals so forecasts stay accurate.

  • Relevant follow-up. Every contact should add value or move to a clear next step. Repeating the same pitch reduces engagement.

Tool: four qualification questions

  1. What business challenge are you trying to solve right now?

  2. What impact is this challenge having on your business?

  3. Who is involved in the purchasing decision?

  4. What budget and timeframe have you set for addressing it?

Turning a prospect into an opportunity

  1. Use discovery to understand the customer's problem.

  2. Explore impact, urgency and the cost of doing nothing.

  3. Identify the stakeholders and their role in buying.

  4. Confirm the need fits your solution.

  5. Agree clear next steps.

Practice

Define the ideal prospect for one of your offers. Choose three prospecting channels and explain why each fits. Then rank three real or imagined prospects by value and explain your order.

Self-check

  1. Which opportunity deserves top priority: high value with no confirmed need, moderate value with a clear need and timeline, or low value with an urgent deadline? Moderate value with a clear need and a defined path to a decision.

  2. What makes follow-up effective? Relevant information, addressing open concerns and agreeing a clear next step.

Day 2 · Module 5: Consultative & Relationship Selling

Consultative selling means understanding the customer's situation before recommending anything, and acting as a trusted advisor rather than a product presenter.

What you will learn

  • The principles and value of consultative selling

  • How to build trust and credibility throughout the sales process

  • Questioning and active listening techniques that uncover real needs

  • The difference between stated requirements and underlying needs

  • How to link needs to solutions and measurable outcomes

  • How to build long-term relationships through ongoing value

Key ideas

  • Start with the customer. Begin with their context, goals and priorities; base recommendations on needs, not assumptions.

  • Trust is earned through behaviour: relevant expertise, transparency, never promising what you cannot deliver, and following through.

  • Four types of question. Open questions let customers describe their situation. Probing questions explore causes, impact and urgency. Clarifying questions confirm unclear requirements. Strategic questions surface overlooked risks and opportunities. Keep it a conversation, not an interrogation.

  • Active listening. Listen for priorities, concerns, emotion and tone. Do not plan your pitch while the customer talks. Summarise to confirm understanding.

  • Stated needs say what; underlying needs say why. A request for staff training may really be a need to raise productivity or reduce errors.

  • From requirement to business value: revenue, cost, efficiency, risk or customer experience. Specific outcomes beat generic product claims.

  • Stakeholder-centred selling. Users care about usability, technical teams about integration and risk, leaders about strategic impact, finance about cost and return. Adapt to each while keeping one value message.

Tool: from discovery to recommendation

  1. Reconfirm the key needs, challenges and priorities.

  2. Link the solution directly to what you discovered.

  3. Explain the expected outcomes and value.

  4. Support it with evidence and examples.

  5. Check that the customer sees the connection.

Five discovery questions to start with

  • What challenges are you currently experiencing?

  • What is having the greatest impact on performance?

  • What have you tried so far?

  • What would you like to achieve?

  • How would you measure a successful outcome?

Practice

Role-play a discovery meeting with a partner. One plays a client with only a general request; the other uses the five questions, summarises what they heard, and names the underlying need before suggesting any solution.

Self-check

  1. A client explains a problem and pauses. What do you do first? Listen, clarify and explore the underlying requirement before offering a solution.

  2. What builds a long-term relationship after the sale? Regular contact, monitoring outcomes, solving issues early and finding new ways to add value.

Day 2 · Module 6: Personality, Behavioural Styles & Client Psychology

People differ in how they communicate, process information and decide. Reading those differences, without stereotyping, lets you adapt your approach and build rapport faster.

What you will learn

  • How behaviour shapes client decisions

  • How to spot behavioural patterns without stereotyping

  • How to recognise communication and decision styles

  • How to read verbal and non-verbal cues

  • How to identify client motivations and drivers

  • How to adapt your approach to each client

Key ideas

  • Patterns, not labels. Some clients decide fast, others need time to reflect. Treat styles as observable patterns, never as permanent personality labels.

  • Observable signals: pace, the kind of questions asked, need for detail, focus on outcomes or on relationships, and changes in engagement. Read signals together, and explore rather than assume.

  • Verbal cues. Repeated questions may signal uncertainty or risk; short answers may mean time pressure; emphasis on cost or timing reveals priorities.

  • Non-verbal cues such as expressions, posture and engagement give context, but culture shapes them. Use them as prompts to ask, not as proof.

  • Motivations and drivers: growth, efficiency, security, control and risk. Different stakeholders in the same company can be driven by different things, and drivers shift with role and buying stage.

  • Pressure and change. Clients may react with more questions, hesitation, resistance or avoidance. Stay composed, explore the reason, and reduce uncertainty with structure and evidence.

  • Ethics first. Use psychology to understand and help the client, never to manipulate.

Tool: adapting to three common styles

Fast pace, direct questions, wants results nowConcise, outcome-focused information and quick decisionsLead with outcomes, timeline and next steps
Cautious, detailed questions, slow to commitEvidence, logic and time to evaluateGive structured evidence, assumptions and risks

Keep the core value proposition the same for everyone; adapt only the pace, detail and emphasis.

Practice

Think of three clients or colleagues you work with. For each, write the behaviour you have actually observed, the preference it suggests, and one change you will make in your next conversation. Then write one question you could ask to check your assumption.

Self-check

  1. A cooperative client goes quiet after a major change is introduced. What is the best interpretation? A possible concern to explore, not proof of lost interest.

  2. How do you pitch to an analytical, a relationship-focused and a results-focused stakeholder at once? Address evidence, people impact and outcomes within one coherent message.

Day 3 · Module 7: Persuasion, Influence & Advanced Objection Handling

Customers decide with both logic and feeling. Ethical persuasion helps them see value clearly, and good objection handling finds the real concern behind what they say.

What you will learn

  • What drives customer decisions

  • How to apply ethical persuasion that builds trust

  • How to balance rational value with emotional factors

  • How to find the real reason behind an objection

  • How to reframe objections around customer priorities

  • How to use storytelling to influence and drive action

Key ideas

  • The psychology of decisions. Customers weigh benefits against risks and possible losses. Value rises when the solution is relevant and credible, and confidence reduces the uncertainty that delays commitment.

  • Ethical persuasion backs claims with evidence and realistic expectations, uses relevant examples as social proof, and respects the customer's choice. No deception, no false pressure.

  • Rational and emotional factors together. Rational: price, return, performance, quality, risk. Emotional: trust, confidence, security, fear of a wrong decision. For example: "This cuts processing time by 20% and gives your team confidence managing peak workload."

  • Business storytelling. Start with a challenge the customer recognises, show how it was addressed, and end with a specific, realistic outcome. Emotion supports the logic; it does not replace it.

  • Ethical urgency comes from real deadlines, risks, costs or market shifts. Show the cost of delay, back it with facts, and let the customer decide. False scarcity destroys trust.

  • The real objection. "Too expensive" may hide doubts about value or budget; timing concerns may signal approvals or priorities; trust objections come from limited evidence. Question and listen before you respond.

  • Competitor objections are a chance to learn the customer's criteria. Compare on what they value and on total value, never by criticising the competitor.

Tool: the five-step reframe

  1. Acknowledge the concern.

  2. Explore what is driving it.

  3. Offer a broader, relevant perspective.

  4. Support it with evidence or an example.

  5. Let the customer reassess with the full picture.

Questions that uncover the real objection

  • "What specifically concerns you about the investment?"

  • "What impact would implementation have on your current operations?"

  • "What challenges are you experiencing with your current approach?"

Practice

Write down the three objections you hear most often. For each, note the likely underlying concern, one clarifying question, and a reframed response linked to a business outcome. Rehearse them with a partner.

Self-check

  1. A prospect says "Your solution is too expensive." What do you do first? Ask whether the concern is total price, budget, perceived value or something else.

  2. Which is ethical urgency: a fake price rise tomorrow, or a genuine deadline and cost of delay? The genuine deadline and cost of delay.

Day 3 · Module 8: Strategic Negotiation & Closing

Negotiation is more than discussing price. It is a structured process for reaching an agreement that creates enough value for both sides, and closing turns that agreement into clear commitments.

What you will learn

  • The principles, stages and outcomes of business negotiation

  • How to prepare a strategy based on customer priorities, value and your limits

  • Value-based techniques for handling pricing and contractual pressure

  • How to manage concessions while protecting profitability

  • How to recognise negotiation styles and buying signals

  • Closing techniques that secure commitment and clear next steps

Key ideas

  • Prepare before you negotiate. Define your desired outcome, minimum acceptable position and objectives; know the customer's priorities, budget and decision criteria; decide what can move and what is protected; have your evidence and alternatives ready.

  • Value-based negotiation keeps the conversation on what the customer gains: revenue, cost savings, efficiency, reduced risk, better experience. Strong value positioning reduces dependence on discounts.

  • Explore pricing pressure first. It can come from budget limits, competitor comparisons, procurement policy or tactics. Is the price really too high, or is the value unclear? Flexibility can come from scope, phasing, payment terms or service levels instead of price.

  • Concessions are planned and conditional. Decide them in advance and always ask for something in return: commitment, volume, timing, scope or payment terms. Unconditional concessions invite more requests.

  • Win-win means separating positions from interests and finding trade-offs where each side gains something it values.

  • Difficult tactics such as aggressive demands, silence or false urgency call for composure, clarifying questions and clear boundaries.

  • Buying signals: questions about implementation, delivery, payment terms, documentation or next steps, and talk of internal approvals. Several signals together are stronger evidence than one.

Tool: the five-step close

  1. Confirm the customer's key needs and concerns are addressed.

  2. Summarise the value, benefits and key commercial terms.

  3. Use a summary close when the customer is clearly aligned.

  4. Use an alternative-choice close when two practical next steps can help them decide.

  5. Confirm the decision, responsibilities, timeline and immediate next action.

Practice

A client likes your proposal but asks for a 15% discount, more flexibility on timing and extra early support. Write two conditional concessions, for example extra support in exchange for a longer contract, or phased delivery in exchange for faster payment, and plan how you will open the conversation.

Self-check

  1. A client opens by demanding 15% off before you know their priorities. What do you do first? Understand their priorities, constraints and perceived value before discussing concessions.

  2. The client asks about start dates and contract terms. What does that signal? Buying signals: confirm remaining concerns and ask for commitment.

Day 3 · Module 9: High-Impact Business & Sales Pitching

A winning pitch quickly shows why the subject matters to this audience, connects their problem to your solution and its value, and moves them from attention to confidence to action.

What you will learn

  • The structure and essential elements of a high-impact pitch

  • How to analyse the audience and decision context before you build the pitch

  • How to structure a pitch around problem, solution, value and outcomes

  • How to open powerfully and use storytelling and evidence

  • How to show executive presence through voice, body language and delivery

  • How to handle questions, objections and challenging audiences with composure

Key ideas

  • Know the audience first: their roles, priorities, authority, concerns and objectives. Finance, operations, technical and senior stakeholders judge the same proposal differently. Anticipate their questions.

  • A powerful opening starts with something that matters to them: a business problem, an insight, a question or a statistic. Create curiosity without exaggeration and make the purpose clear quickly.

  • Problem, solution, value. Define a problem worth solving, show how the solution answers it, translate it into outcomes the audience values, and back the link with evidence. Keep the three connected throughout.

  • Credibility comes from relevant experience, data, case examples and results, chosen to answer the audience's concerns. Make specific, realistic claims and admit limitations.

  • Storytelling: situation, challenge, response, outcome. Stories support facts; they never replace them.

  • Persuasive visuals: one clear purpose per slide, charts when they beat words, no decoration that distracts, consistent layout.

  • Executive presence: know your subject, stay natural, use posture, eye contact, pace and pauses deliberately, and read the room.

  • Handling questions: hear the full question, clarify vague ones, answer the specific concern, use evidence when value is challenged, and never become defensive.

Practice

Prepare a three-minute pitch for one of your offers to a mixed audience of a marketing lead, a finance director and a sales director. Use the planner, deliver it to a partner, and agree one improvement based on their reaction.

Self-check

  1. Why did previous pitches to senior teams fail when they were too detailed? They focused on activities instead of business outcomes, hiding the overall value.

  2. How do you adapt one pitch to several stakeholders? Change the emphasis and evidence for each, keep one core value message.

Day 3 · Module 10: Pitch Mastery, Client Simulation & Action Planning

The final module brings everything together: you prepare and deliver a pitch in a realistic client simulation, handle objections live, move to a clear next step, and leave with a personal action plan.

What you will learn

  • How to apply advanced pitching in realistic client situations

  • How to deliver concise, persuasive individual and business pitches

  • How to adapt structure, language and delivery to personalities and stakeholders

  • How to handle objections and unexpected situations with control

  • How to integrate negotiation and closing into the pitch

  • How to build a personal plan to keep improving

Key ideas

  • Prepare an individual pitch: know the client, their challenge and desired outcome; define the one message they must remember; keep only evidence that supports the decision; rehearse; know the exact action you want at the end.

  • Read the client during the pitch. More questions can mean interest or a need for proof; changes in tone or posture can signal concern. Treat signals as prompts, adjust pace and detail, and keep your structure.

  • Handle objections in the moment: listen fully, acknowledge, clarify, link your answer to their priorities, use evidence, stay composed, and confirm the concern is resolved before moving on.

  • From pitch to close: recognise buying signals, explore remaining concerns, protect value before any concession, confirm readiness, and secure a specific next step with owners and timing. Never end with "we'll be in touch".

  • Manage time and focus. Prioritise the messages that drive the decision and skip detail that will not change it.

  • Improve through feedback. Use specific observations, pick a few high-impact improvements, practise them in realistic scenarios, and track progress.

Tool: the elevator pitch in five steps

  1. Problem: start with a relevant customer challenge.

  2. Solution: show how your offer responds to it.

  3. Value: name the most important outcome.

  4. Difference: give a real reason to choose you.

  5. Next step: invite further discussion.

Practice: client pitch simulation

You have 15 minutes with a client choosing between several agencies. The marketing director wants quick evidence of results, the finance manager challenges the investment, and the business owner wants confidence in communication and support. Structure your pitch, adapt it to all three, answer at least one objection, and close with a specific next step.

Self-check

  1. A direct, results-focused client is cautious without evidence. How do you open? With the most important business outcome, backed by concise evidence.

  2. What is a good way to end a pitch? Summarise the agreed value and confirm a specific next step, who is involved and when.
