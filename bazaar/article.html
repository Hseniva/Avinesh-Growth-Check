Walk through the Port Louis market on a Saturday morning and you'll see it straight away.

Vendors calling out prices. Regulars haggling. Trust built one sale at a time, face to face.

Now open TikTok. You'll see the exact same thing. A live seller holds up a dress, calls out a price, takes orders in the comments and haggles in real time. Same energy. Same instincts. Different screen.

That's what's happening in Mauritius right now, and I don't think there's a better way to describe it: we're building a digitalised bazaar. Not a retail media ecosystem. Not yet, anyway.

Understanding that gap is the key to understanding where e-commerce in Mauritius is really heading. Let's break it down.

Key takeaways

  • E-commerce in Mauritius is small but growing: about US$36 million in 2025, with 5 to 10% growth expected in 2026.
  • Retail media is the advertising layer inside online shopping. Mauritius isn't there yet, but the foundations are forming.
  • Mobile payments, led by MCB Juice (420,000+ subscribers, 7 million+ transactions a month), quietly built the readiness for online buying.
  • Most local social sellers are running a digitalised bazaar: great hustle, but little structure, service or brand.
  • Platforms alone won't win. Service, reliability and trust will.

The growth story: small market, real momentum

Let's start with the numbers, because they tell a story of quiet but real momentum.

According to ECDB, e-commerce revenue in Mauritius reached roughly US$36 million in 2025, after growing 10 to 15% on the year before. The projection for 2026 is another 5 to 10%.

That's not Amazon scale. It was never going to be, in a market of around 1.3 million people.

But it's not nothing either. Mauritius has crossed a threshold. There's now enough activity that brands, platforms and regulators all have to pay attention to how buying and selling really happens online here.

Which brings us to a term you'll hear a lot more over the next few years: retail media.

What retail media actually means (and why it's not just "e-commerce")

In Mauritius, "e-commerce" is the word everyone knows. It means buying things online instead of in a shop. Fair enough.

Retail media is the next layer on top of that. It's the advertising that happens inside the shopping experience itself:

  • The sponsored product that appears first when you search an online store
  • The banner on a supermarket app pushing this week's promotion
  • The "recommended for you" carousel that's really a paid placement

In short, brands pay the platform you're already shopping on to reach you at the exact moment you're deciding what to buy.

Why does this matter for Mauritius? Because retail media is the business model that turns an online shop into a real advertising business. It's what funds the ad empires of the world's biggest retailers.

But to get there, a market first needs the plumbing: payments, logistics and trust, all working together at scale. And that's exactly where the Mauritian story gets interesting.

What quietly powered the shift: the MCB Juice effect

Ask people what made online buying normal in Mauritius and they won't say "e-commerce platforms."

They'll point to their phone. More specifically, to mobile payment apps, and MCB Juice has been the biggest force behind that shift.

MCB Juice now has more than 420,000 subscribers and processes over 7 million transactions every month, according to Peach Payments, one of its payment partners. For a population of around 1.3 million, that's critical mass.

No cash. No card terminal. A QR code on the counter and a few taps on a phone.

Here's my take: payment infrastructure is almost always the unsung hero of e-commerce growth, and Mauritius is a textbook case.

You can build the best online shop in the world, but if people don't trust digital payments or don't know how to use them, none of it matters. Juice, along with similar moves from SBM and other banks, quietly built the readiness that e-commerce, and eventually retail media, can now build on.

The digitalised bazaar: where Mauritius really is today

The stats only tell half the story. Here's the other half.

Spend some time watching how Mauritian sellers operate, especially the wave of TikTok live sellers, and you'll notice something.

Most of them aren't running e-commerce businesses in the structured, branded sense. They're running a bazaar stall, rebuilt on a digital stage. Same instincts: price it, call it out, sell it live, move fast.

What's missing is the layer that turns a stall into a brand:

  • Consistent service and clear delivery times
  • Simple returns and after-sales support
  • A repeatable customer experience
  • Something you can trust without having watched the seller live

You can see this gap clearly in one telling fact: TikTok Shop isn't available in Mauritius. It currently runs in around 20 countries, and none of them are in Africa (PhotonPay).

So sellers improvise. They take buyers from a TikTok live straight into WhatsApp to close the sale by hand. Some even register a business abroad just to get access to commerce tools.

That's not a small workaround. It's a market assembling its own infrastructure in real time, seller by seller, deal by deal.

Why platforms alone won't be the thing that wins

It's tempting to think the fix is obvious: Mauritius needs its own Amazon. One big, central marketplace.

I've actually turned that idea over in my own head, something like an "Amazon Mauritius". And the more I think about it, the more I land on one conclusion: the platform was never the hard part.

Amazon didn't win because it built a website. It won because of warehouses, delivery networks, return policies people trust and customer service that shows up when something goes wrong. That's also where the real cost and risk of a local marketplace would sit: logistics, inventory and service, not the website.

The same goes for the big supermarket brands in Mauritius. Their strength isn't their checkout page. It's years of a name people already trust when they walk into the physical store.

For a market this size, a full Western-style retail media model, with sponsored listings, ad auctions and data-driven targeting, isn't realistic in the short term. The volume isn't there yet.

But the lesson applies at any scale: service, reliability and consistency are what separate a real e-commerce business from a live bazaar stall on an app. Any serious player, platform or individual seller, wins or loses on that. Not on how slick the shopping cart looks.

What's about to reshape the playing field

Two things are worth watching closely.

1. Taxing foreign digital players

Ultra-cheap imports from platforms like Temu and AliExpress have squeezed local merchants who carry real costs: rent, staff and local taxes.

The 2026-2027 Budget proposes taxing non-resident ICT companies that serve Mauritian customers. If the pricing gap with foreign platforms narrows, it changes the game for local sellers who struggle to compete on price alone.

2. Mauritius Post and the parcel economy

Mauritius Post is a quiet but telling example. It has had to move from letters to parcels to keep up with online shopping, and the same import and tax shifts reshaping retail will reshape it too.

It's a small example with a big message: even the infrastructure layer of Mauritian e-commerce is still being rewritten.

Before you buy: how to protect yourself

I've put this section last on purpose. Not as an afterthought, but because it's the part you should act on once you've read everything above.

As more buying happens through informal social sellers rather than registered businesses, one truth is easy to forget: if something goes wrong with an unregistered seller, you often have no real recourse. No registration to chase. No formal complaints process. Just a hope that they reply to your message.

Here's what to do:

  1. Know where to report. MAUCORS, the Mauritian Cybercrime Online Reporting System, lets you report online scams and fraud. CERT-MU, the national computer emergency response team, publishes alerts and advice.
  2. Check who you're buying from. A registered business has a paper trail and accountability. A friendly social seller, however responsive in the comments, often doesn't.
  3. Pay in a traceable way. Keep your payment reference for every purchase.
  4. Use your bank. If a purchase goes wrong, contact your bank's dispute team quickly with your transaction details.

Frequently asked questions

What is retail media?

Retail media is advertising inside a shopping platform, such as sponsored products in search results, promoted banners in a supermarket app or paid "recommended for you" placements. Brands pay the retailer to reach shoppers at the moment they decide what to buy.

How big is e-commerce in Mauritius?

According to ECDB, e-commerce revenue in Mauritius was about US$36 million in 2025, with 5 to 10% growth projected for 2026. It's a small market, but growing steadily.

Is TikTok Shop available in Mauritius?

No. TikTok Shop runs in around 20 countries and none of them are in Africa. Mauritian sellers usually sell through TikTok lives and close orders on WhatsApp instead.

How can I report an online scam in Mauritius?

You can report it on MAUCORS, the Mauritian Cybercrime Online Reporting System. CERT-MU also shares alerts and advice. If you paid by bank or mobile payment, contact your bank quickly with your transaction reference.

The bottom line

The digitalised bazaar is exciting. It's where a lot of Mauritius' e-commerce energy lives right now.

The next step is structure: service, reliability and trust that turn hustle into real businesses. The sellers and brands that build that layer first will own the market when retail media finally arrives.

And as a buyer? Shop the bazaar. Just shop it with your eyes open.
